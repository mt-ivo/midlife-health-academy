Could It Be My Hormones?

Your Menopause & Perimenopause Questions Answered

Waking up at 3am? Feeling anxious for no obvious reason? Struggling with brain fog, dry eyes, aching joints or changes in your sex life?

You're not imagining these changes, and you're certainly not alone.

Perimenopause and menopause can affect far more than your periods. Changes in hormone levels can influence your physical health, emotional wellbeing and everyday life.

At MidLife Health Academy, we believe understanding your hormones is the first step towards making informed decisions about your health.

Here are answers to some of the questions women frequently ask.

1. Sleep, Mood & Brain Fog

Why do I keep waking up at 3am during perimenopause?

Do you fall asleep easily but suddenly find yourself wide awake at 3am?

Sleep disturbances are common during perimenopause and menopause. Fluctuating hormones, night sweats, anxiety and changes in sleep patterns can all contribute.

Although waking at 3am isn't a specific diagnostic sign of menopause, hormonal changes may be one factor. Stress, alcohol and sleep disorders can also disrupt sleep.

Understanding what is happening in your body can help you explore appropriate ways to improve your sleep.

Can perimenopause cause anxiety even if I've never been anxious before?

Yes. Some women experience new or worsening anxiety during perimenopause, even if they've never previously struggled with their mental health.

Hormonal fluctuations, disrupted sleep and the pressures of midlife may all contribute.

Symptoms can include excessive worrying, feeling overwhelmed, irritability and physical sensations such as a racing heart.

However, new anxiety shouldn't automatically be attributed to hormones. Other causes may need consideration.

Why am I forgetting words and struggling to concentrate?

Brain fog is a commonly reported symptom of perimenopause and menopause.

You might forget familiar words, struggle to concentrate, lose your train of thought or walk into a room and forget why you're there.

Changes in hormone levels, sleep disruption and stress may contribute to these difficulties.

Persistent or progressive memory problems should be medically assessed rather than automatically attributed to menopause.

Can menopause cause depression or low mood?

Hormonal changes during perimenopause can be associated with low mood, tearfulness, irritability and depressive symptoms.

Sleep problems and difficult life circumstances can make these feelings worse.

There are effective treatment options, including psychological therapies and, where appropriate, HRT. Clinical depression may require its own assessment and treatment.

Why am I exhausted even after a full night's sleep?

Fatigue is frequently reported during perimenopause and menopause.

Even when you've spent eight hours in bed, night sweats, interrupted sleep and changes in mood may affect sleep quality.

However, persistent exhaustion can also be caused by anaemia, thyroid disorders, sleep apnoea or other health conditions.

Understanding the possible causes is important before deciding how best to manage fatigue.

2. Unexpected Menopause Symptoms

Can menopause cause dry eyes?

Yes. Dry eye symptoms may become more common around menopause.

Hormonal changes can influence tear production and the quality of the tear film that protects the surface of your eyes.

You may notice gritty, burning, watery or uncomfortable eyes.

Dry eyes have several possible causes, including medications and eyelid conditions. Persistent symptoms should be assessed by an optometrist or appropriate healthcare professional.

Why are my ears suddenly itchy during perimenopause?

Some women report itchy ears during perimenopause and menopause.

Hormonal changes may contribute to changes in skin moisture and sensitivity, although direct evidence linking itchy ear canals specifically to menopause is limited.

Eczema, dermatitis, infection and irritation from products are other possible causes.

Persistent itching, discharge, pain or hearing changes should be assessed.

Can perimenopause cause heart palpitations?

Some women experience palpitations during perimenopause and menopause.

They may feel like a racing, fluttering or unusually noticeable heartbeat.

Hormonal fluctuations, anxiety, caffeine and disturbed sleep may contribute.

However, palpitations should not automatically be blamed on menopause. Recurrent or persistent symptoms warrant medical assessment. Palpitations accompanied by chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness require emergency care.

Why do my joints ache during menopause?

Joint and muscle pain are recognised symptoms associated with menopause.

Some women notice stiffness, aching or discomfort in their shoulders, hands, knees or other joints.

Oestrogen has roles in musculoskeletal health, and changing hormone levels may contribute to symptoms.

Regular physical activity and strength training can support joint function and muscle health. Persistent pain, swelling or significant stiffness should be assessed for other causes.

Can menopause cause a burning mouth or changes in taste?

Some women experience burning sensations in their mouth, oral dryness or altered taste around menopause.

Hormonal changes may play a role, although the relationship is not fully understood.

Burning mouth symptoms can also be associated with nutritional deficiencies, medication effects, oral conditions and other medical problems.

Persistent symptoms should be assessed by a dentist or healthcare professional.

3. Sexual Health & Vaginal Changes

Why has sex suddenly become painful during menopause?

Painful sex can develop during perimenopause or menopause, even if you've never experienced it before.

Falling oestrogen levels can affect the tissues of the vagina and vulva, making them thinner, drier and less elastic.

This is part of a condition known as genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM).

Treatment options include vaginal moisturisers, lubricants and local vaginal oestrogen.

Painful sex should never simply be accepted as an inevitable part of ageing.

Why have I lost my sex drive during perimenopause?

Changes in sexual desire are common during menopause.

Hormonal changes, vaginal discomfort, poor sleep, stress, relationship factors and emotional wellbeing can all influence libido.

There isn't always one simple explanation.

Treatment depends on the underlying causes and may include addressing vaginal symptoms, reviewing medication, psychological support or discussing hormonal treatment options.

Can menopause cause recurrent urinary tract infections?

Yes. Lower oestrogen levels can affect the tissues surrounding the vagina, urethra and bladder.

These changes may increase susceptibility to recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) after menopause.

NICE recommends considering vaginal oestrogen for recurrent UTIs in appropriate peri- and postmenopausal patients.

Urinary symptoms should still be assessed because not every episode of burning or urgency is caused by an infection.

Does vaginal oestrogen really work?

Vaginal oestrogen is an evidence-based treatment for vaginal and urinary symptoms associated with menopause.

It can improve vaginal dryness, irritation and discomfort during sex.

Unlike systemic HRT, vaginal oestrogen acts mainly locally, with minimal absorption into the bloodstream.

It can be used by many women who are already taking HRT, and serious side effects are very rare. Individual medical circumstances should still be considered.

Is vaginal dryness normal during perimenopause?

Vaginal dryness is common during perimenopause and after menopause, but that doesn't mean you have to live with it.

Changes in oestrogen can affect vaginal moisture, elasticity and tissue health.

Symptoms may include dryness, burning, irritation and discomfort during sex.

Effective treatments are available, including non-hormonal vaginal moisturisers and local vaginal oestrogen.

4. Weight, Skin, Hair & Long-Term Health

Why am I gaining belly fat in my 40s?

Many women notice changes in their body shape during midlife, particularly increased fat around the abdomen.

Ageing, changes in muscle mass, activity levels, sleep and hormonal changes can all contribute.

Menopause is associated with a shift towards greater abdominal fat storage, although weight gain itself has several causes.

Strength training, regular activity, adequate protein and balanced nutrition can help support long-term metabolic health.

Does menopause cause sagging skin and wrinkles?

Oestrogen plays a role in maintaining skin collagen, hydration and thickness.

As oestrogen levels decline, skin may become drier, thinner and less elastic.

However, skin ageing is also strongly influenced by sun exposure, genetics and lifestyle.

Daily sun protection, appropriate skincare and healthy habits remain important. HRT should not be prescribed solely as an anti-ageing skin treatment.

Can menopause cause hair thinning?

Hair thinning can occur around menopause.

Changes in the balance of hormones, together with ageing and genetic factors, may affect hair growth and density.

Other causes include iron deficiency, thyroid disorders, stress and certain medications.

Sudden, patchy or significant hair loss should be assessed rather than assumed to be hormonal.

How does menopause affect bone health?

Oestrogen helps maintain bone strength.

As oestrogen levels fall after menopause, bone loss can accelerate, increasing the risk of osteoporosis and fractures.

Weight-bearing exercise, resistance training, adequate calcium, vitamin D and appropriate medical treatment can all support bone health.

HRT can also help prevent bone loss and reduce fracture risk while it is being taken.

Does menopause increase my risk of heart disease?

Cardiovascular risk generally increases with age, and menopause is associated with changes in cholesterol, body fat distribution and vascular health.

However, heart disease risk depends on many factors, including blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, physical activity and family history.

Menopause is a useful opportunity to review cardiovascular health and prevention.

HRT is not routinely recommended solely to prevent cardiovascular disease.

5. HRT, Oestrogen, Progesterone & Testosterone

Is hormone replacement therapy (HRT) safe?

For many women experiencing troublesome menopausal symptoms, HRT is an effective treatment.

It can improve hot flushes and night sweats and help prevent bone loss.

Like all medicines, HRT has potential benefits and risks. These vary according to age, medical history, the type of hormones used and how they are taken.

The decision should be individual, informed and regularly reviewed.

Does HRT increase breast cancer risk?

The relationship between HRT and breast cancer depends on the type of treatment.

Combined oestrogen and progestogen HRT is associated with an increased risk of breast cancer, which generally increases with longer use.

Oestrogen-only HRT has a different risk profile and is generally used following a total hysterectomy.

Understanding absolute risk, alongside personal and family history, is important when making treatment decisions.

Can I take HRT if I'm still having periods?

Yes. You do not need to wait until your periods stop before discussing HRT.

Some women experience significant perimenopausal symptoms while continuing to menstruate.

The most appropriate treatment depends on symptoms, bleeding patterns, medical history and whether contraception is required.

HRT itself is not contraception.

What does testosterone do for women during menopause?

Testosterone is a hormone naturally produced by women as well as men.

NICE recommends considering testosterone for low sexual desire associated with menopause when HRT alone has not been effective.

Evidence does not currently support routinely prescribing testosterone for brain fog, fatigue, weight loss or general wellbeing.

Treatment should be individually assessed and appropriately monitored.

Is it ever too late to start HRT?

There is no single age at which every woman must stop or cannot start HRT.

However, the balance of benefits and risks can change with age and the number of years since menopause.

Starting systemic HRT after age 60 or more than 10 years after menopause generally requires particular consideration of cardiovascular and other risks.

An individual discussion with an appropriately qualified healthcare professional is essential.

6. Understanding Perimenopause & Finding Support

How do I know if I'm perimenopausal?

Perimenopause is the transitional stage leading up to menopause, when hormone levels fluctuate and symptoms may develop.

You may notice changes in your periods, hot flushes, night sweats, mood, sleep or vaginal health.

In otherwise healthy women aged 45 and over, perimenopause is usually identified through symptoms and menstrual history rather than routine hormone blood tests.

Other causes of symptoms may still need investigation.

What age does perimenopause usually start?

Perimenopause commonly begins in the 40s, although the timing varies.

The average age of natural menopause in the UK is around 51.

Menopause is defined retrospectively after 12 consecutive months without a period, provided there is no other explanation and hormonal contraception is not masking bleeding patterns.

Symptoms can begin several years before the final period.

Do I need a hormone blood test to diagnose menopause?

Not necessarily.

For otherwise healthy women aged 45 and over with typical symptoms, NICE advises that perimenopause and menopause are generally identified clinically.

Hormone levels fluctuate considerably during perimenopause, so a single blood test may be misleading.

Blood tests may be appropriate in certain circumstances, particularly when menopause is suspected before age 45 or another condition needs investigating.
